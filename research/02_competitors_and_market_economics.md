# 02 — Competitors and Market Economics (US third-party residential property management)

Research date: 2026-11-21. Scope: competitive landscape for Asset Living and the economics/operating model of third-party ("fee") management. Asset Living's own profile, the PMS/proptech vendor landscape, and Asset Living's pain points are covered in separate documents.

## How to read this document

Every material claim carries one of three labels:

- **[V] Verified** — taken from a primary source (NMHC, NAA, FTC, Fannie Mae, a company's own release) or from named trade-press reporting that I fetched and read. "Verified" means "the source says this", not "independently audited".
- **[VC] Vendor claim** — published by a technology vendor (EliseAI, Funnel, AppFolio, Buildium, Zego, Bilt) or by a company about itself in marketing copy. Self-reported, unaudited, usually without a control group. Treat as directional.
- **[I] Inference** — my own arithmetic or interpretation. Not a sourced fact.

A fourth tag, **[S] Snippet-level**, marks figures that came from a search-result summary where I could not open the underlying page (paywall, 403, or unparseable PDF). These need re-checking before they go in front of a client.

Research constraint: the session's web-search budget ran out roughly three-quarters of the way through. The sections on HOA/BTR competitors, manager profit margins, owner-churn frequency, cost of a unit turn, and KPI benchmarks are thinner than intended. Everything affected is listed in "Gaps / unverified" at the end.

### A2. Competitor profiles

Ownership notes are marked where I did not verify them in this pass.

#### Greystar (No. 1; 1,014,091 units)

- **Scale and verticals [V]:** 933,396 market-rate, 27,574 BTR, 30,910 student units (NMHC 2026). Also on NMHC's top owner, developer and builder lists. About 3,700–4,000 communities under US property management (Bisnow, 2025-09-02, https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/top-talent/greystar-exec-toni-eubanks-on-tech-transformation-and-transparency-130785). A vendor recap cites 250+ markets and 700+ clients **[VC]** (Funnel, 2025-05-07, https://funnelleasing.com/today-in-operations-centralization-in-action/).
- **Ownership:** privately held, led by founder Bob Faith (CEO per NMHC). Ownership structure not verified in this pass.
- **Growth mode [V]:** organic plus "lift-out" acquisitions of other managers' books — e.g. Grand Peaks (Denver), about 11,000 units in seven states, September 2025 (Multifamily Dive, 2026-04-10, URL above).
- **Centralization [V]:** "Resource Center" programme centralizes accounts receivable and accounts payable for about 500 properties; rent collection is being centralized. Leadership frames repetitive functions as the candidates for centralization or AI and is openly sceptical that a chatbot replaces on-site work ("you can't necessarily accomplish that with a chatbot") (Bisnow, 2025-09-02).
- **Pods [VC]:** region-centric and sometimes client-centric pods handle centralized admin and leasing for defined property groups. Pod counts and staffing ratios are not disclosed (Funnel, 2025-05-07).
- **AI stack [VC]:** Funnel's CRM/AI live on 180,000+ Greystar units — about 18% of the portfolio **[I]**. Reported effects of AI delinquency notices: up to 10 hours saved per person per week and payments arriving about six days earlier (Funnel, 2025-05-07). A Greystar executive called "AI native to CRM" a key lesson; 60% adoption of AI-suggested responses with staff review (Funnel panel recap, https://funnelleasing.com/panel-recap-from-reactive-ai-to-truly-agentic-systems/) **[S]**.
- **AI organization [S]:** job postings for a "Director, AI Adoption and Operations Integration" and reference to a corporate AI SWAT team (https://jobs.greystar.com/job/arizona/director-ai-adoption-and-operations-integration/35302/95719348256). Undated.
- **Third-party constraint, in Greystar's own words [V]:** COO Andrew Livingstone — "There is a natural risk aversion among institutional owners"; earliest centralization adopters were larger properties (NAA/20for20, 2025-09-25, https://naahq.org/news/centralization-harder-third-party-managers-some-are-doing-it-anyway).
- **Regulatory exposure [V]:** $24M FTC/Colorado settlement over fee advertising (2025-12-02, https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/greystar-agrees-pay-24-million-stop-deceptive-advertising-practices-result-ftc-colorado-lawsuit); $50M share of a $141M RealPage class settlement (AP, 2025-10-03, https://finance.yahoo.com/news/greystar-other-landlords-agree-141m-191233342.html); separate DOJ settlement in August 2025 (https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/multifamily/greystar-reaches-deal-with-doj-will-cooperate-in-realpage-antitrust-case-130508) **[S]**.
- **Read [I]:** the scale leader, cautious and process-led on AI, vendor-partnered rather than building its own leasing AI, and carrying the heaviest regulatory baggage. Its centralization coverage is still a minority of the portfolio.

#### Willow Bridge Property Company (No. 3; 244,457 units)

- **Scale and verticals [V]:** 237,123 market-rate, 9,163 BTR (NMHC 2026). Also an NMHC top developer.
- **Ownership [S]:** formerly Lincoln Property Company's residential division, rebranded October 2023. Cadillac Fairview (Ontario Teachers') took a 49% stake in 2019 and later acquired the business; sources conflict on whether that closed in February 2023 or 2024 (https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/lincoln-residential-acquisition-willow-bridge/696466/; https://dallasinnovates.com/lincoln-propertys-residential-division-rebrands-as-willow-bridge-following-its-february-acquisition/).
- **Technology [V]:** after-hours AI chatbot for prospects with human pickup next day; two staff dedicated to vetting and implementing new technology; explicit preference for tools that integrate with the existing PMS rather than adding software load (Bisnow Chicago panel, 2025-11-13, https://www.bisnow.com/news/chicago/multifamily/how-ai-is-reshaping-chicago-multifamily-operations-131886). Appears as an EliseAI client logo **[VC]** (https://eliseai.com/abm/bridge).
- **Not found:** any published centralization programme, pod model, or efficiency result.
- **Read [I]:** a fast follower with a light public technology narrative. Least differentiated on AI of the top four, on the public record.

#### RPM Living (No. 4; 241,479 units)

- **Scale and verticals [V]:** 215,180 market-rate, 10,787 BTR, 331 student (NMHC 2026). Describes itself as a full-service management, investment and development company (Bilt release, 2026-11-10, https://newsroom.biltrewards.com/RPM-neighborhoodOS). Ownership not verified.
- **Centralization [VC]:** a "Managed Services" division centralizes applicant screening/decisioning and resident account management, sitting as "a layer of oversight and optimization" over AI. Nearly 50,000 units signed up during the 2025 budgeting season — about 21% of the portfolio **[I]**. Products: EliseAI LeasingAI, Maintenance, Delinquency, VoiceAI. Reported results: occupancy +2%, on-time payments +8%, 186 hours of monthly capacity per community, about $3,700 more delinquent rent collected per community per month, 72% of AI-initiated maintenance requests completed within two days, 91% of communications automated (EliseAI customer story, undated, https://eliseai.com/customer-stories/how-rpm-living-scaled-their-managed-services-offering-with-eliseai).
- **Resident platform [VC]:** expanded pilot with Bilt "Neighborhood OS" — rent payments with rewards, maintenance, packages, access, amenities, plus staff-facing AI agents that can be texted to add move-in tasks or approve bookings (Bilt, 2026-11-10).
- **Stance [V]:** RPM's SVP of software support: AI "isn't one thing"; start from the pain point; it needs ongoing engagement, not one-time setup (MFE Leadership Summit, 2026-03-02, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/business-finance/multifamily-executive-leadership-summit-day-one-takeaways).
- **Read [I]:** the most explicit of the big four about selling centralization as an opt-in, separately packaged service that owners sign up for at budget time. That design sidesteps the chargeback problem described in B2.

#### AMC (No. 5; 156,223 units)

- **[V]** Utah-based; grew "largely organically" — CEO Brenda Barrett: "We've never had one person in our organization that has ever been focused on generating new business for us" (Multifamily Dive, 2026-04-10).
- No public technology or centralization strategy found.

#### Avenue5 Residential (No. 6; 153,968 units)

- **Scale [V]:** 139,040 market-rate, 2,316 BTR (NMHC 2026); 825+ communities (Bilt release, 2026-11-10, cited at https://newsroom.biltrewards.com/Avenue5-neighborhoodOS) **[S]**. Pure third-party manager. Ownership not verified.
- **Centralization result [V]:** moving assistant-manager administrative duties off-site "has cut payroll by 8% to 10%", with stronger collections and renewals reported without figures (EVP Robin Hanson, MFE Leadership Summit, 2026-03-02).
- **AI [VC]:** EliseAI LeasingAI on nearly 50,000 units (about 32% of portfolio **[I]**); reported +3.27% occupancy, +3% NOI, +21.61% lead-to-lease on same-store stabilized properties; consolidation of point solutions made the subscription "close to net-neutral for owners" (https://eliseai.com/customer-stories/reinventing-leasing-at-avenue5-with-eliseai).
- **Stance [V]:** COO Jeff Krohn says Avenue5 wants to lead on AI, with adoption driven through regional managers and above (MFE, 2026-03-02).
- **Read [I]:** the mid-size fee manager with the clearest quantified payroll claim in its own executive's words.

#### Cushman & Wakefield (No. 7; 135,892 units; formerly Pinnacle)

- **[V]** Lost 14,046 units in 2025 and fell from No. 5 (NMHC 2026). Publicly traded global CRE services firm; multifamily is one service line.
- **[S]** Q3 commentary cited "declines in multifamily property management"; Q4 2025 call framed AI as augmenting advisers, stated no planned large headcount reduction, and described an enterprise data "spine" across business lines (https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/02/19/cushman-wakefield-cwk-earnings-transcript/; https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/minimal-impact-from-interest-rates-in-q3-cushman-wakefield/873866/).
- **Read [I]:** the clearest example of a large fee manager shrinking through client attrition.

#### Bozzuto (No. 8; 133,117 units)

- **[V]** Private, family-led (CEO Toby Bozzuto); also an NMHC top builder. Positioned as high-touch/hospitality.
- **Centralization [V]:** property managers cover pods of about four properties with administration handled off-site; "We've been able to realize savings of about $100,000 in payroll" (MFE, 2026-03-02). The scope of that $100,000 (per pod, per region, total) is not stated.
- **AI [V]:** began with a leasing chatbot, extended to maintenance, delinquency follow-ups, resident engagement and applications; flags point-solution fragmentation as an industry problem (MFE, 2026-02-13, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/leadership/multifamilys-ai-moment-still-early-innings-bozzuto-says). Pilot properties saved an average of 32 hours of work; an in-house resident/employee platform pulls upcoming renewals, open maintenance requests and resident details and flags them for proactive outreach (Bisnow, 2025-11-13).
- **Read [I]:** closest public analogue to an "AI layer that surfaces what to do next for on-site teams", built in-house, at about 30% of Asset Living's scale.

#### BH Management Services (No. 13; 94,087 units)

- **[V]** Lost 13,012 units in 2025 (NMHC 2026). Paid $15M in the RealPage class settlement (AP, 2025-10-03). Proprietary in-house analytics platform, "BH Fusion" (Bisnow, 2025-02-24, https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/top-talent/apartments-ai-driven-revolution-creating-tech-haves-and-havenots-128182).
- No centralized-leasing announcement found.

#### Morgan Properties (No. 11 manager; No. 2 owner)

- **[V]** 111,132 units managed; an owner-operator that manages what it owns, not a fee-management competitor for mandates. Largest unit gain on the 2026 owners list (+13,748) (Multifamily Dive, 2026-04-10).

#### Others worth knowing

- **ZRS Management** (No. 10; 113,953; +21,026 in 2025) and **RAM Partners** (No. 16; 85,072) — fast-growing Sunbelt fee managers **[V]** (NMHC 2026). No technology detail found.
- **Bell Partners** (No. 18; -7,021) — its centralization pilot found seven-day on-site operation unnecessary and that the right centralized role varies by property **[V]** (NAA/20for20, 2025-09-25).
- **Public REITs** (MAA, AvalonBay, Equity Residential, UDR, Camden, Essex) are not competitors for third-party mandates, but they set the efficiency benchmark that institutional owners then expect of fee managers. See B2.

- **Student housing [V]:** Yugo (The Dot Group, Dubai) acquired Campus Advantage — about 40,000 beds, 88 properties, 28 states (Multifamily Dive, 2025-09-08, https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/student-housing-management-merger-acquisition/759525/). ### A7. Where Asset Living sits, and how it could differentiate

What the evidence supports:

1. **[V]** Asset Living is a clear No. 2, having grown about 6x since 2020, mostly by acquisition, with a majority-third-party, multi-vertical book.
2. **[I]** On the public record its technology posture looks like its peers': the same vendor (EliseAI is used by Asset Living, RPM, Avenue5, and appears at Willow Bridge), a BI layer, and a shared-services arm. Nothing published distinguishes its AI from RPM's or Avenue5's. The reported "proprietary technology suite" in the New Mountain coverage is not described anywhere I could find.
3. **[I]** Its published centralization coverage (35,000+ units, about 8%) is lower than what vendors report for Greystar (~18%), RPM (~21%) and Avenue5 (~32%). The dates and definitions differ, so this is a weak comparison, but it does not suggest Asset Living is ahead.
4. **[I]** Rapid M&A implies several inherited operating models, client contracts and data estates (FPI, Alpha Barnes, JMG and others), layered over owners who each dictate their own PMS — Asset Living's site explicitly sells expertise across Yardi, RealPage and Entrata.

Differentiation options that follow (all **[I]**):

- **Own the cross-PMS layer.** Peers' AI is vendor-supplied and largely tied to one CRM or one PMS integration. A fee manager that must run on whatever system each owner mandates has a structural reason to own a system-agnostic operating layer; an owner-operator REIT does not. This is the most defensible angle and it matches something Asset Living already claims ("PMS-agnostic data architecture").
- **Make owner retention a product.** Contracts are typically 30-day cancellable (B3), and three large peers lost 7,000–14,000 units each last year. No competitor publicly markets client-churn prediction or owner-facing transparency as a capability. This is open ground.
- **Use multi-vertical data.** Student, affordable and conventional have different leasing cycles, compliance rules and failure modes. An operating layer that handles all three is harder to copy than a conventional-only leasing bot.
- **Convert M&A integration from a cost into a capability.** A layer that sits above acquired books makes the next lift-out faster, which matters to a PE owner whose thesis is consolidation.
- **Avoid competing on "we have a leasing chatbot".** That is commoditised; four of the top six use the same vendor.

## Part B — Industry economics and operating model

### B1. How third-party management makes money

**Base management fee**

- **[V]** A named institutional owner (ZMR Capital) pays managers **2.75% to 3% of monthly gross collections** (rent plus ancillary income) with a **$25 per unit per month minimum**. Another (Vero Sade) pays a percentage of total revenue including ancillary income such as late fees (NAA *Units*, 2023-05-18, https://naahq.org/node/5188).
- **[V]** Fannie Mae underwrites a management fee at the greatest of 3% of effective gross income, the actual fee, or the appraiser's market fee. The floor may drop to **2.5% of EGI** only if the loan exceeds $9M, the underwritten fee is at least **$500 per unit**, and market evidence supports it (Fannie Mae Multifamily Guide §203.01, current 2026 version, https://mfguide.fanniemae.com/node/7526).
- **[S]** Management fees were about 9% of operating expenses for garden properties (NAA 2021 survey) and 8% for mid/high-rise (2020 survey) (https://naahq.org/sites/default/files/2021_ies_executive_summary_final.pdf; https://www.naahq.org/sites/default/files/2020_ies_exec_summary_final.pdf). Management fee expense rose 4.0% in 2023 **[V]** (NAA, https://naahq.org/node/6498).
- **[VC]** The 7–12% of rent often quoted online applies to single-family and small portfolios, not institutional multifamily (e.g. https://www.rentecdirect.com/blog/property-management-fees/).
- **[I]** Working range for institutional conventional multifamily: roughly 2.5–3.5% of collected revenue, lower for very large or high-rent assets, with a per-unit monthly floor. Affordable housing is typically priced per unit per month under agency schedules (HUD publishes regional fee schedules, e.g. https://www.hud.gov/sites/dfiles/Housing/documents/Management_Fee_letter_SW_Region_4-1-2024.pdf — not analysed here).

**Revenue per door — my estimate [I]**

- NAA reports same-store revenue of **$1,545 per unit per month in 2025** **[V]** (via CRE Daily, 2026-10-15, https://www.credaily.com/?p=224105).
- At 2.5–3.0% that is about **$39–$46 per unit per month, or roughly $465–$555 per unit per year**. This squares with Fannie Mae's $500-per-unit condition and ZMR's $25 monthly floor ($300 per year minimum).
- Applied crudely to 446,427 units, base fees would be on the order of **$200–250M per year**. This ignores student (priced per bed), affordable (different basis), fee minimums and ancillaries. It is an order-of-magnitude figure only. I found no disclosed Asset Living revenue.
- **The implication is the important part:** a manager earns about $500 a year on a unit whose on-site payroll costs the owner several times that. One percentage point of occupancy is worth far more to the owner than to the manager's fee line. Manager economics therefore depend on (a) not losing the contract, (b) corporate overhead per door, and (c) ancillary services.

**Pass-throughs and ancillaries**

- **[V]** On-site staff costs are charged back to the property; the owner pays them. 20for20's analysis is explicit that managers who "charge property-level associate costs back to owners" have not really centralized, and that property management agreements assigning staff to specific properties lock in the decentralized model (NAA, 2025-09-25).
- **[VC]** Asset Living's own ancillary lines include a client-exclusive insurance programme (50,000+ units enrolled), an in-house marketing agency (Poetic, 1,200+ property websites), construction management (capacity for $250M of active projects), lease-up services (300+ lease-ups in 24 months), due diligence, centralized AP and centralized recruitment (https://assetliving.com/property-management/services).
- **[V]** Owners are leaning on "other income" at property level — Centennial Holdings called 2026 "our year of other income", citing bulk internet (MFE, 2026-03-02). NAA reported other income up 8.3% in 2023 (https://naahq.org/node/6498).
- Not verified: typical market rates for construction-management fees, lease-up fees, technology fees, or how centralized services are priced.

**Margins**

- **[S]** Executives from third-party managers described margins as lower than in the past and contracts as typically 30-day cancellable (Multi-Housing News, "Performance is Paramount", https://www.multihousingnews.com/performance-is-paramount/ — page returned 403; snippet only).
- **I could not find any disclosed operating or EBITDA margin for a large US multifamily fee manager.** All are private or buried inside larger public companies. Do not quote a margin figure without a source.

**Where the owner's rent dollar goes (context) [V]**

NAA's 2025 breakdown, from 13,471 communities (about 1.7M units): debt service 43 cents; operating costs including insurance, utilities, upkeep and management fees 27; property taxes 10; **payroll 7**; capital reserves 1; owner about 11. Same-store revenue rose 2.7% while operating cost rose 4.6% to $8,213 per unit per year (via CRE Daily, 2026-10-15).

### B2. Staffing norms and what centralization changes

**The traditional rule**

- **[V]** "For at least 20 years, many in the apartment industry considered a ratio of one team member for every 100 units the standard for onsite staffing" (NAA, 2022-04-28, https://naahq.org/node/4436). NAA's best-practice material puts it as one office and one maintenance employee per 100 units **[S]** (https://www.naahq.org/sites/default/files/naa_bestpractices_satffing.pdf).
- **[V]** The same NAA article argues the ratio is unsustainable and recommends one team member per **45–61 units**. An NAA survey average of one FTE per 45 units is also cited **[S]**.
- **[VC]** AppFolio calculates a US average of 54 rental units per property-management employee (range 35 in Salt Lake City to 77 in New Orleans), using BLS Q4 2024 employment and Census unit counts. This includes corporate staff and owner-operators, so it understates frontline load (2025-10-31, https://www.appfolio.com/blog/market-update-3q2025).
- **[I]** A 300-unit conventional community therefore typically carries 5–7 on-site staff, split roughly evenly between office and maintenance.

**What centralization looks like in practice**

- **[V] AvalonBay:** "neighborhood model" groups up to **5 properties or 1,500 units under one manager**; a shared service centre has existed for 17 years; payroll was expected to grow about 1% in 2024 against average merit increases of about 4%, "due to a reduction in on-site positions" (Multifamily Dive, 2024-09-03, https://multifamilydive.com/news/equity-residential-avalonbay-udr-artificial-intelligence-centralization-leasing/725923). Targets **$80M of annual incremental NOI** from operating initiatives; 60% achieved; about $7M more in 2026. Structure is tiered: self-service and AI, centralized associates, neighbourhood teams (MFE, 2026-04-13, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/technology/avalonbay-turning-tech-centralization-and-ai-operating-gains).
- **[V] Equity Residential:** headcount down **20% between 2020 and 2025**, attributed to centralization and AI leasing, citing a 2026-02-27 investor presentation (Bisnow, 2026-06-22 and 2026-07-31). Management expects "another 5% to 10% reduction in on-site payroll over the next several years" and is deploying a new CRM (MFE, 2026-02-25, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/business-finance/apartment-reits-enter-2026-transition-mode-banking-supply-relief). Its AI assistant handled 600,000 inquiries and booked 80,000+ appointments in 2023; pods share resources across roughly 600–700 units (Multifamily Dive, 2024-09-03).
- **[V] UDR:** 30+ properties with no on-site staff (2024); credits its customer-experience project with a 300 bps reduction in turnover (Q1 2025) (Multifamily Dive, 2024-09-03 and 2025-06-02, https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/apartment-resident-retention-centralization-turnover/749567/).
- **[V] Third-party managers:** Avenue5 — 8–10% payroll reduction from centralizing assistant-manager administration; Bozzuto — one manager across about four properties (MFE, 2026-03-02); Greystar — AR/AP centralized at about 500 properties (Bisnow, 2025-09-02).
- **Camden and MAA:** I found no specific centralization or headcount figures. MAA's CEO referred to "efficiency gains from technology initiatives rolling out in '26 and beyond" **[S]**.

**Why it is harder for a fee manager than for a REIT [V]** (20for20 via NAA, 2025-09-25, based on interviews with 14 third-party managers and 10 institutional asset managers)

- The owner, not the manager, decides whether a property changes operating model, and it is negotiated one property at a time.
- Changing the model means renegotiating the management agreement and replacing staff chargebacks with a fee for shared services.
- Owners want proof that leasing and resident experience will not suffer.
- Third-party portfolios are geographically scattered, so pods are harder to form.
- Only 5 of 14 managers interviewed offered centralized services as standard to every client and bid; "some of the largest operators are not centralizing".
- It is easier to go from four front-office staff to three than from three to two — larger properties go first.
- Remaining site staff often feel they have picked up extra work when a role is centralized (Greystar COO).

**Vendor claims on staffing impact [VC]**

- EliseAI's 262-page playbook: AI can handle about 60% of the leasing process, cut leasing and administrative hours by 40%, save 10–20% of payroll and improve NOI by 20% a year. It advises telling on-site teams about workload changes on go-live day, and says savings come from not backfilling attrition rather than from layoffs (Bisnow, 2026-06-22). EliseAI says its software is used at one in six US apartments.
- An NAA AI survey, as reported by Bisnow, found 10% payroll savings, a 15% retention increase and a 5% satisfaction improvement; 57% of companies use AI but only 6% across multiple functions (Bisnow, 2025-02-24). Original NAA document not checked.

**Regional manager span of control**

- Not benchmarked in any source I found. One job posting describes a senior regional manager over 6–10 Class A assets and about 2,000 units **[S]**. One owner calls the regional manager "a lynchpin position", ideally with a capped portfolio (NAA *Units*, 2023-05-18) **[V]**.
- **Manager-to-door ratios for Asset Living or peers are not public.** This is the central metric for the prototype's value case and has to come from Asset Living itself.

**Payroll as a share of property operating expense**

- **[S]** Salaries and personnel: about 25% of operating expenses for garden properties (NAA 2021 survey), 21% for mid/high-rise (2020 survey). Old, and from a survey series NAA says is not comparable with its later Income/Expense IQ.
- **[V]** 2025: payroll is 7 cents of each rent dollar, against 27 cents for other operating costs and 10 for property tax (NAA via CRE Daily). **[I]** That puts payroll at about 16% of total operating cost including taxes (7 of 44), or about 21% excluding taxes (7 of 34). On $1,545 monthly revenue, payroll is roughly $1,300 per unit per year.
- **[S]** Combined administrative and payroll expense was $2,323 per unit in 2024, up 3.81% on 2023 and nearly 20% on 2021 (attributed to NAA; secondary source https://stealthagents.com/research/multifamily-property-management-staffing-costs-2026). The $1,300 and $2,323 figures come from different samples and definitions and should not be combined.
- **[V]** Payroll expense rose 5.6% in 2023 (NAA, https://naahq.org/node/6498). "Payroll is the budget category where operators have the most control" — EliseAI playbook **[VC]**.
- **[I]** Working figure: payroll is the largest controllable line, roughly a fifth to a quarter of controllable operating expense, about $1,300–$2,300 per unit per year depending on definition. A 10% payroll saving is therefore worth roughly $130–$230 per unit per year to the owner — a quarter to a half of the manager's entire base fee on that unit.

**On-site staff turnover and its cost**

- **[V]** Total turnover of on-site property-level employees: **29.2%** over the 12 months to 2025; **33.4%** at organizations with 450+ employees; voluntary turnover 23.4% (up from 21.7%); corporate roles 14.2% (RCLCO 2025 survey via NAA, 2026-04-01, https://naahq.org/news/why-employee-retention-challenges-go-deeper-wages).
- **[S]** A higher figure of 36–45% for Q4 2024 is attributed to NAA/NMHC by J Turner Research (2025-05-20, https://www.jturnerresearch.com/blog/looking-into-the-leaders-survey). Different method; original not located.
- **[V]** Direct replacement cost: "nearly $5,000 per new hire" (Grace Hill calculator, cited by NAA, 2026-04-01) — excludes lost productivity and resident impact. This is a vendor tool's estimate.
- **[I]** For a firm of Asset Living's size — if it employs on the order of 8,000–10,000 on-site staff, which is my assumption from one per ~50 units and not a sourced figure — 30% turnover at $5,000 is roughly $12–15M a year in direct replacement cost alone.
- **[V]** Workforce sentiment (NAA Voice of the Property Manager, survey July 2024, 850+ property and regional managers, https://naahq.org/node/6515): top challenges are abusive residents (22%) and inability to disconnect after hours (16%); 34% report poor work-life balance; **68% are reluctant to change property management software within 12 months**; 23% expect fewer property managers in five years because of centralization or technology; AI training is the biggest training gap.
- **[V]** Q4 2025 job postings for core apartment roles fell 13.8% year over year (NAA, https://naahq.org/apartment-labor-market-dynamics-report/q4-2025).

### B3. Churn, sense 1 — owner/client churn

**What is established**

- **[S]** Third-party management contracts are "typically 30-day cancellable", which is why "performance is paramount" (Multi-Housing News Q&A with Greystar, Pennrose and Gables executives; snippet only).
- **[V]** Unit counts swing sharply: Cushman & Wakefield -14,046, BH -13,012, Bell -7,021 in 2025 while ZRS added 21,026 and Willow Bridge 23,781 (NMHC 2026). Greystar wins whole books through lift-outs.
- **[V]** What institutional owners say they look at when choosing or keeping a manager (NAA *Units*, 2023-05-18): rents, occupancy, income growth, expenses and turn times against same-vintage submarket peers; collections; resident retention; local scale and in-place talent; staff retention; vendor pricing; **reporting capabilities**; reimbursement schedule; lender approval; quality and capped workload of the regional manager. Crisis performance (collections and virtual leasing during COVID) was cited as a sorting event.
- **[V]** Institutional owners are risk-averse about operating-model change and want evidence it will not harm leasing or experience (Greystar COO, NAA, 2025-09-25).
- **[VC] Small-portfolio owners** (Buildium, 300 owners, 95% with 1–20 units, fielded Feb–Mar 2026, https://www.buildium.com/blog/how-rental-owners-evaluate-a-property-managers-performance/): reasons for switching — poor communication 57%, declining service quality 54%, lack of transparency 34%. Metrics used — response time 43%, maintenance turnaround 31%, occupancy 30%, time to lease 25%. 43% expect a same-day response; owners want self-service dashboards.
- **[VC]** AppFolio (1,009 owners with 1–25 units, 2026): 73.6% would consider switching over an inadequate online experience **[S]** (https://www.appfolio.com/blog/owner-preferences-report-2026-findings).

**What I could not establish**

- **No source quantifies annual client or unit churn for institutional third-party managers**, or the share of management changes triggered by asset sales versus dissatisfaction.
- That buyers commonly install their own preferred manager on acquisition is standard industry practice, and purchase contracts commonly require the seller to deliver a termination of the existing management agreement at closing **[S]** (contract examples at https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/termination-of-management-agreement/_8). I found no frequency data.
- The small-owner survey findings should **not** be presented as institutional evidence. The institutional equivalents of "communication, service quality, transparency" are plausible but unproven here.

**[I] Working model of why an institutional owner fires a fee manager**

1. Asset sold; buyer brings its own manager. Not controllable, but partly recoverable if the manager is on the buyer's approved list.
2. Underperformance against submarket comps on occupancy, rent growth, collections, controllable expense.
3. Site or regional staff turnover that visibly damages a property.
4. Reporting that is late, inconsistent, or requires the asset manager to chase.
5. Loss of the relationship — the regional or VP the owner trusted leaves.
6. Post-merger disruption at the manager.

Items 2–5 produce signals in operational data weeks or months before a termination notice. Item 6 is live for Asset Living.

### B4. Churn, sense 2 — resident churn

**Retention and renewal levels**

- **[V]** 63% of renters renewed in 2025, against 62% in 2024 (RentCafe, via search summary of https://www.credaily.com/briefs/lease-renewals-surge-as-renters-avoid-housing-market) **[S]**.
- **[VC]** Managers targeted 63% retention in 2025 but averaged about 58%; 58% of renters say they plan to renew; renewal intent rises with tenure — 52% under one year, 64% over five years (Zego, 1,000+ renters and 600 managers, via MFE, 2025-06-10, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/property-management/zego-report-reveals-disconnect-between-property-managers-renters-on-retention-priorities_o). Zego's 2026 report puts average retention at 57% **[S]**.
- **[V]** REIT turnover, Q1 2025: Camden 31% annualized (among its lowest ever); Essex 35%; MAA 41.5% (down from 46% two years earlier); Equity Residential 7.9% for the quarter (its lowest ever). Historically about half of renters did not renew; major landlords were at 30–35% in Q1 2025 (Multifamily Dive, 2025-06-02; Bisnow, 2025-05-21, https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/multifamily/renters-are-staying-put-at-higher-rates-than-normal-129420).
- **[V]** The main reason retention is high is macro, not operational: the cost of owning versus renting, mortgage rates and economic uncertainty (same sources). **Do not credit an AI tool for retention that the housing market delivered.**

**Cost of a turn**

- **[V]** Direct operating and maintenance cost: **$750–$1,800 per unit**, before marketing and vacancy loss (Bainbridge executive, Bisnow, 2025-05-21).
- **[S]** The widely quoted "about $4,000 per turn" is attributed to NAA by vendor blogs (e.g. https://www.theguarantors.com/blog/owners-and-operators/boost-resident-retention-and-your-bottom-line-proven-tips); another figure in circulation is $2,725. I could not trace either to a primary NAA publication. Use "$2,500–$4,000 all-in, commonly cited, unverified".
- **[I]** At $1,545 monthly rent, each vacant week costs about $355. Three weeks of vacancy plus $1,000–$1,800 of make-ready and some marketing and concession cost lands at roughly $2,500–$4,000, which is consistent with the quoted range.
- **[I]** At 250 units, moving retention from 55% to 58% avoids about 7–8 turns a year — roughly $20–30K. Worthwhile for the owner; small relative to a payroll change.

**Drivers of move-outs**

- **[VC]** Renters' top reasons for not renewing: rent too expensive, **poor maintenance service**, safety, poor upkeep, **poor management responsiveness**. Managers instead cite life changes, home purchase and moving somewhere more desirable. Maintenance, safety and upkeep "rank at the top of the list for renters and at the very bottom for property managers" (Zego via MFE, 2025-06-10). Renters start reconsidering at an average 8% increase **[S]**.
- **[VC]** Renters satisfied with maintenance were 57% more likely to plan to renew (Buildium 2026, 1,500 renters); 81% of those satisfied with repairs are more likely to renew, and 53% of dissatisfied renters reported resolution taking weeks or longer (AppFolio 2026, 3,002 renters) **[S]** (https://www.buildium.com/resource/2026-renters-report/).
- **[V]** Operator testimony links service to retention: UDR's customer-experience project and the 300 bps turnover reduction; MAA credits customer service and its Google review scores; Equity Residential credits its centralized renewal process (Multifamily Dive, 2025-06-02). American Landmark's CEO calls resident and employee retention "one of the most effective cost-control strategies" (MFE, 2026-01-29, https://www.multifamilyexecutive.com/apartment-trends/inside-2026-operations-playbook).
- **[I]** The link between maintenance responsiveness and renewal is consistently reported but rests almost entirely on vendor surveys of stated intent plus executive assertion. I found no independent causal study. Present it as "strongly indicated", not "proven".

### B6. Macro trends, 2024–2026

**Supply wave and rent softness**

- **[S]** Completions peaked in 2024 at a roughly 40-year high (700,000+ units); about 595,000 expected in 2025 and about 450,000 in 2026 (Yardi); starts at their lowest since 2012 (RealPage) (https://www.apartments.com/grow/learning-center/supply-vacancy-outlook-2026; https://www.costar.com/article/871425367/what-to-watch-in-2026-gradual-recovery-on-tap-for-us-multifamily-market — CoStar page not directly readable).
- **[V]** Rents fell in 2025 for the first time since the financial crisis and ended the year flat; concessions were at their highest since then; consensus for 2026 is about 2% (Multifamily Dive, 2026-01-14, https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/rent-outlook-2026-multifamily-apartment/809477/). Multifamily starts fell more than 40% between 2023 and 2025.
- **[V]** Sunbelt REITs felt it most: MAA 2025 same-store revenue -0.1%, NOI -1.4%; Camden revenue +0.8%, NOI +0.3%. Camden: supply "has peaked and is falling like a knife in our markets" (MFE, 2026-02-25).
- **[I]** Asset Living's Texas and Sunbelt weighting puts it where rent softness was worst. Flat rents mean flat percentage-based fees, while the manager's own costs rose.

**Expense pressure**

- **[V]** Operating costs rose 4.6% in 2025 against revenue of 2.7%; the owner's share of the rent dollar fell to about 11 cents (NAA via CRE Daily).
- **[V]** Insurance: $502 per unit in 2021 to $777 in 2024 (+55%); the Federal Reserve estimates about $821 in 2024, up about 77% since 2019; some Houston properties exceed $1,200 per unit; insurance was 4.78% of revenue in 2024 against 1.95% in 2000 (NAA, 2026-03-18, https://naahq.org/news/premium-pulse-national-multifamily-insurance-cost-acceleration). **[S]** Premiums eased 6.2% in the year to Q1 2026 but remain about 60% above five years earlier (Yardi via NMHC).
- **[V]** 2023: insurance +26.2%, repairs and maintenance +11.8%, administrative +11.7%, property tax +9.7%, payroll +5.6% (NAA, https://naahq.org/node/6498).
- **[V]** Vendors are passing 5–10% price increases to owners (Greystar, Bisnow, 2025-09-02).

**Centralization and AI**

- Covered in A2 and B2. Two further points. **[V]** More than half of operators in 20for20's research had centralized administration at some communities, but most had not moved leasing roles off-site (via Funnel, 2025-05-07). **[V]** The next phase is described as extending centralization to maintenance, vendor management and procurement, and consolidating around platforms rather than "layering new point solutions onto already complex ecosystems" (MFE, 2026-01-27, https://multifamilyexecutive.com/technology/3-tech-trends-set-define-2026).
- **[V]** The labour story is now public and adversarial: Bisnow headlines include "AI adoption jeopardizes half a million property management jobs" (466,100 property, real estate and community association managers per BLS 2024) and coverage of a vendor playbook that coaches operators on how to communicate headcount reduction (Bisnow, 2026-07-31 and 2026-06-22).

**Application fraud**

- **[V]** NMHC/NAA pulse survey (75 large operators, fielded Nov 2023–Jan 2024): 93.3% experienced fraud in the prior 12 months; 70.7% saw an increase in fraudulent applications or payments; average bad-debt write-off $4.2M (median $800K); on average 24.5% of bad debt (median 15.8%) attributed to fraudulent applications (https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240124537737/en/Rampant-Increasing-Fraud-Impacting-Rental-Housing-Costs) **[S — figures from search summary of the release]**.
- **[V]** Greystar: in some markets up to 50% of applicants fail screening as fraudulent; costs include marketing, screening and staff time touring fake applicants (Bisnow, 2025-09-02).

**Regulation: fee transparency**

- **[V]** FTC and Colorado v. Greystar: $24M (2025-12-02). Greystar must disclose the total monthly leasing price whenever it advertises base rent, and disclose every fee, its purpose and whether it is mandatory before taking any payment (FTC release, URL above).

- **[V]** Operational consequence: Greystar needed to push pricing changes through roughly 4,000 point-of-sale systems and integrations (Bisnow, 2025-09-02).

**Regulation: algorithmic pricing**

- **[V]** DOJ–RealPage proposed settlement (announced about 2025-11-24): no nonpublic competitively sensitive data shared among landlords in rent recommendations; end of such market surveys; removal or redesign of features that limit rent decreases or align competitors; a court-appointed monitor; no fine and no admission (ProPublica, 2025-11-26, https://www.propublica.org/article/doj-realpage-settlement-rental-price-fixing-case). Court approval status not verified.
- **[V]** Class action: 26 property managers settled for more than $141M — Greystar $50M, BH $15M, Simpson $6.5M, others $0.55M–$6M; the case continues against RealPage and non-settling firms (AP, 2025-10-03). **Whether Asset Living or FPI is a defendant or settling party was not checked.**

## Implications for an AI-first operating layer

All of this section is **[I]** — my reading of the evidence above.

1. **The buyer's economics are about overhead per door and contract retention, not property NOI.** A fee manager earns roughly $500 per unit per year. Property-level savings accrue to the owner. The prototype's value case to Asset Living must therefore be framed as (a) more doors per regional and corporate FTE, (b) fewer lost contracts, and (c) chargeable shared services — with owner NOI gains as the proof that keeps clients, not as Asset Living's own return.

2. **Owner churn is the least-served problem and the best fit for a demo.** Contracts are 30-day cancellable; three large peers lost 7,000–14,000 units in one year; and no competitor publicly offers client-risk detection. A module that scores each owner relationship from operational signals — performance against submarket comps, reporting lateness, site-staff turnover, open asset-manager requests, regional-manager changes — and prompts action is differentiated and directly tied to the stated goal of reducing customer churn.

3. **Being PMS-agnostic is the moat, not a feature.** Fee managers run whatever system each owner mandates. Competitors' AI is vendor-supplied and bound to particular integrations, and 68% of site managers do not want to change PMS. A layer that normalises data across Yardi, RealPage and Entrata and acts on top of them fits Asset Living's reality and its existing claim.

4. **Design for the chargeback problem.** The documented blocker to third-party centralization is commercial: staff are charged to properties, and each owner must agree to change. The operating layer should make centralized work measurable and attributable per property and per owner — tasks handled, hours released, outcome deltas — so it can be priced as a service and evidenced at budget time. RPM's opt-in "Managed Services" model is the precedent.

5. **Target the work that has already been shown to move.** Evidence is strongest for administrative and assistant-manager tasks: collections follow-up, AR/AP, renewals administration, screening, lead response. Avenue5's 8–10% payroll figure and Greystar's Resource Center are both in this zone. Fully remote leasing and unstaffed properties are REIT territory and a harder sell to third-party owners.

6. **Raise regional-manager span of control.** No benchmark exists publicly, owners call the regional manager the linchpin, and Bozzuto's in-house tool (surfacing renewals, open work orders and resident context for proactive outreach) is the only peer example. An exception-driven portfolio view for regionals — what needs attention today, across systems — maps directly to "manager-to-door" efficiency.

7. **Treat maintenance responsiveness as the retention lever, but claim it carefully.** Renters rank it at the top; managers rank it at the bottom. Work-order triage, communication and escalation are credible targets. The supporting evidence is vendor surveys and executive testimony, and current high retention is mostly macro-driven, so the demo should show the mechanism rather than promise a renewal uplift.

8. **Stay out of rent-setting.** With four state laws, a DOJ settlement and $141M of class settlements, any feature that recommends rents using cross-owner nonpublic data is a legal liability — and a third-party manager sitting across hundreds of competing owners is exactly the fact pattern regulators describe. Operational intelligence across owners is fine; pricing coordination is not. Make the boundary explicit in the design.

9. **Build fee transparency and auditability in.** The Greystar order requires total-price disclosure everywhere a price appears and fee-by-fee disclosure before payment. A layer that can assert and log what was shown to whom, across many systems, addresses a current compliance burden.

10. **Fraud screening is a credible adjacent module.** 93% of large operators report fraud and it drives a material share of bad debt. It is measurable and uncontroversial.

11. **Handle the workforce narrative deliberately.** The trade press is now covering AI in property management as a jobs story, and site staff already report that centralization leaves them with more work. On-site turnover near 30% means capacity can be released through attrition rather than layoffs, and reducing after-hours load and abusive-contact exposure addresses the top two complaints managers report. The demo should show capacity released and where it goes.

12. **Position against the point-solution sprawl.** Executives at Bozzuto, Willow Bridge and in MFE's 2026 outlook all complain about fragmented tools. An "operating system" pitch lands only if it reduces the number of surfaces staff touch.

13. **Use M&A integration as a selling point.** Asset Living added about 158,000 units in a year and is mid-transition between PE owners. A layer that gives one operating view over acquired books before systems are merged has immediate internal value and supports the acquirer's thesis.
